Step 1 of 10

Context

I was lead designer on Composer through GA and worked closely with engineering.

I had to collaborate with many cross-organizational teams who had ideas about what Composer could be. This enabled me to lead with product managers in making decisions about scope, milestones, and timelines for Composer features that I would then help relay to other teams and help them prioritize their designs and roadmaps to contribute to Composer. This also led to writing design principles for Composer so that designers could always freely explore and improve Composer, but ensured we were telling a consistent story throughout the product.

I started working on Composer in February 2026. We released Composer to more and more customers while we improved it, and it reached GA at the end of June.

Step 2 of 10

Problem and stakes

Composer is the next version of Marketing Agent, built as a prompt box. Marketing Agent assumed what customers wanted to make. Composer lets them say.

Marketing Agent lessons applied

I started with a prompt box so customers could say what they want right now. Composer also lives where the work is, not only in a header. Both lessons came straight from Marketing Agent.

Step 3 of 10

Customer voice

I talked to customers every week, often several times. I started with interviews and moved to usability tests within the same session. When we would ship a milestone to production, I ran rounds of beta feedback with those customers in the trial while simultaneously testing the next milestone as well. It was one loop: research, define, iterate, ship, test.

Customers kept asking to see their data. Generating something was great, but they wanted to know why we generated something for them. They asked for more than generated content. They wanted numbers like expected lift, comparisons with similar companies, signals from their own recent campaigns, and a warning when a flow still carried an old logo. They wanted a partner that checked their work and backed it with data.

Showing what changed

Users also wanted to know what we were updating, since we shipped changes every day. I opened a PR myself that pulled from an internal changelog and published it to Composer's public releases.

Step 4 of 10

Constraints

Scope was the biggest constraint. People see a chat box and assume it can do anything. We had to be useful with what it could do and transparent about what it couldn't. After Composer created a campaign, for example, you couldn't edit it through chat yet, so we implemented a disclaimer in Klaviyo editors.

Step 5 of 10

Decisions

The idea behind Composer: AI is always there for you and never forced on you.

Composer is a companion, not the whole product

Composer starts as your companion. It could become the primary workspace, but it doesn't have to. I think AI should come to the user and not become the entire product, especially in Klaviyo, which isn't a natural-language product. Composer is part of how you do your work, not a replacement for it.

A decision I reversed: suggestion chips

I disliked them at first. Generic chips like "create a campaign" or "automate a flow" felt impersonal in a world full of personalization. But, users had a hard time knowing what Composer could do, and chips helped them learn so we reimplemented them.

At launch, the suggestion chips follow the page: the flows page suggests ways to use flows, and the campaigns page suggests ways to use campaigns. That keeps people in the work they planned for the day.

What I chose not to build

We chose not to let any Composer widget become a mini application. My co-designer led that decision after a lot of back and forth with executives about Composer's scope, and I was part of those conversations as lead designer.

My argument was working with and without AI. People should be able to switch between the two and make small tweaks to a campaign, flow, or form without hopping between an AI product and everything else. I didn't want a widget to become a new interface. Instead, we should work harder to update the current interface and let widgets help the user use that interface instead.

Unifying overlapping AI capabilities

Composer pulled together AI features that already lived in Klaviyo, like the subject line assistant, Marketing Agent content generation, and Flows creation. This is where I worked across the organization most, with the PMs, engineers, and designers who owned each area. Composer calls their technology so results stay consistent, and whatever those teams add shows up in Composer.

I designed Composer so teams can grow and shape the individual areas of their product to let Composer adapt to them instead of the other way around.

Step 6 of 10

The role of consultant

PMs, engineers, and leadership came to me with different ideas, asks, or advice. Examples that came up again and again:

  • Is this consistent with the other features? For example, do the different agents contributing to Composer handle error states the same way?
  • How will people discover Composer in my area? I worked with each contributing team to find places in their part of the product where Composer could open naturally. Getting Composer into those spaces was a big part of reaching GA.
Step 7 of 10

When AI doesn't have the answer

At GA, instead of "I don't know how to do that," Composer says it can't do this yet and that we're working on it. Then it offers a next step: "What edits would you like to make? I can walk you through how to do that." When it can't do the work, it walks you through doing it yourself.

Step 8 of 10

How I defined quality

Part of quality with Composer was consistency of how the chat responded. For the first release I helped set concrete output criteria like no emojis and tables render as tables. I also pushed for short recommendation lists that consist of only three recommendations at a time with more on request, so people weren't overwhelmed.

Another part of quality was how customers were using and responding to the chat content. Our eval tool recorded the sentiment of customer conversations. I could flag phrases like "that's not it," "that's wrong," and "no, you're wrong," and all-caps typing. Those flags led us to the frustrated conversations, which we read to find the pain points. The hardest part of looking at evals was synthesis. Pulling every finding together and deciding what to fix first and get it on the roadmap is always a challenge across teams, with PMs, engineers, and leadership.

Step 9 of 10

Outcome

At GA, customers were satisfied but expected Composer to tackle more challenges and grow fast. We were quick to conduct more user tests and interview sessions to improve on our next release of Composer.

Step 10 of 10

What I'd do differently

I'd bring people in earlier. We didn't realize how many teams would contribute to Composer at the beginning and once every team became its own agent, figuring out how to get everyone involved while features were being pushed to production every day was challenging.
