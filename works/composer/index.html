Step 1 of 10

Context

I was the lead designer on Composer through GA, working closely with engineering. Another lead designer picked up a piece of the components, but I designed the first iteration of nearly everything customers saw at GA.

I was also the strategic voice people consulted. When designers or PMs had an idea, they talked it through with me to decide whether it made it into the product.

To keep all of that consistent, I created design principles for Composer: a shared narrative that still let designers explore, while keeping the product consistent and well rounded.

I started on Composer in February 2026. From February to July we released it to more and more customers as we improved the product, and it reached GA at the very beginning of July.

Step 2 of 10

Problem and stakes

Composer was the next iteration of Marketing Agent: a prompt box model. Instead of us coming to customers and assuming what they wanted to create, Composer gave them control over what to create.

Marketing Agent lessons applied

Marketing Agent decided what to make for customers, so in Composer I started with a prompt box and let customers say what they want right now. Composer also appears where the work is, not in a header. Both came straight from what Marketing Agent taught us.

Step 3 of 10

Customer voice

I talked to customers every week, often several times a week. I started with interviews, then moved into usability tests. Once we could ship to production, I ran two rounds of beta feedback, one for each beta release, while testing the next phase with users at the same time. It was a constant loop: research, define, iterate, test.

One thing I kept hearing: customers expected Klaviyo to show them their data. It was great that we could generate something, but they wanted to know why it would work. They asked for expected lift, for comparisons with similar companies, for signals from their own recent campaign performance, or a heads-up that a flow hadn't been updated since they changed their logo. They wanted a partner that made sure everything was clean and backed by data.

Customers especially wanted to know how their flows were performing, so I suggested an audit. I didn't design it myself. I mentored the designer and PM who did, and it became one of Composer's skills. My push was that an audit can't stop at numbers. Every number we show has to be something a customer can go change, so the audit also has to say what I would change because of it. The first release gave those recommendations in plain text.

Step 4 of 10

Constraints

The biggest constraint was scope. When people see a chat box, they assume it can do everything right away. We had to do enough to be useful and design around what it couldn't do yet, and say so plainly. For example, once Composer created a campaign, you couldn't edit it through the chat yet.

Time was the other constraint, and with it the risk of not meeting what users expected, especially for editing. Letting the chat read the page was another big gap. I pushed for it and got it on the roadmap by our second beta release. Once Composer knew which page the user was on, a recommendation could tell them which page to go to when they weren't on it yet.

Step 5 of 10

Decisions

The idea behind Composer: AI is always there for you, but it's never forced on you.

How I decided what got in

When someone asked whether Composer should do something, the answer was almost always yes. If it helps users take an action, helps them learn more, or makes Composer a real assistant for their everyday tasks, it belongs.

That includes some questions outside Klaviyo. "What's the weather like today?" matters if it's 75 degrees in the middle of November and you want to run a flash sale to clear out your warmer clothes. The only things that push back are requests that are derogatory or not OK under policy. Drawing those lines is hard, and refining them is ongoing.

Decision log

Composer starts as your companion. It could become the primary workspace, but it doesn't have to. I believe AI should come to the user, not become the entire product, especially in a product like Klaviyo, which isn't a natural-language product. Composer becomes a true part of your workspace and of how you carry out your work.

The decision I reversed was suggestion chips. I didn't like them at all at first. Generic chips like "create a campaign" or "automate a flow" felt personal to no one. But users had a hard time knowing what Composer could do, and chips helped show them. So they stayed, as long as they were personal to the space the user was working in.

I pushed for suggestions personalized to each user and got closer, but not all the way. At launch, suggestions are relevant to the page the user is on: the flows page suggests ways to use flows, and the campaigns page suggests ways to use campaigns. The principle is to keep people in the work they planned for the day. The next step is for Composer to take the lead, for example suggesting a campaign after auditing a flow.

What I chose not to build

We chose not to let the Composer widget turn into a mini application. My co-designer led that decision after a lot of back-and-forth with executives about Composer's scope. As Composer's lead designer, I was part of that conversation, and it ties back to the same idea: Composer isn't the main place you work. It's your companion, and it navigates as much or as little of the product for you as you want.

My main driver was working with and without AI. Users should be able to switch between the two, making small tweaks to perfect a campaign, flow, or form without jumping between an AI product and everything else. Klaviyo becomes AI-powered, but AI isn't the only way of working.

Unifying overlapping AI capabilities

Composer brought together many AI features that already existed in Klaviyo, like the subject line assistant, content generation, and auditing. This is where I worked most across the organization, with the PMs, engineers, and designers who owned those areas. Composer used their technology so results stayed consistent, and any contributions those teams wanted to make showed up in Composer.

I wanted Composer to grow with Klaviyo and the other teams, not make them adapt to me. So I designed it so teams could help grow Composer to work with them, not for them.

Path to GA

To reach GA, we cut a lot of the work on bringing AI to the user. We ran out of time and didn't have the resources to get there.

Step 6 of 10

Who came to me

I mentored the designer who owned Composer's brand voice and tone. We reviewed her work often, I helped her write the principles she used with other teams, and I helped her bring those teams together to talk about it.

PMs, engineers, and leadership all came to me, with different asks. Two came up again and again:

  • Is this consistent across the other features? For example, are we handling error states the same way across the different agents that contribute to Composer?
  • How will people discover what Composer can do? I worked with each contributing team to find places in their part of the product where Composer could fit in seamlessly and open the chat. Getting Composer into their spaces, not just a chat box, was a big part of reaching GA.

Reusable principles

I wrote the design principles for Composer. They're for internal use, so I don't share them here, but they became the strategic voice for how Composer should work in Klaviyo. Designers worked from them, and PMs referred to them too.

Step 7 of 10

When the AI was wrong

It's OK for Composer to say "I don't know," but when it does, it should always recommend a next step: what to do, how to follow up, or where to find the data if it isn't in the user's Klaviyo account. A user who is trying to figure something out should never hit a dead end. The only natural end is the end of a conversation.

Composer's limits were different from Marketing Agent's. When people see a prompt box, they want it to do everything, like an assistant that takes over the browser and does the work for them. Composer couldn't do that. For example, a user might ask Composer to edit their welcome series flow, which it couldn't do yet.

Instead of "I don't know how to do that," at GA Composer is transparent: it can't do this right now, but we're working on it. Then it offers the next step: "What edits would you like to make? I can walk you through how to do that." Even when Composer can't do the work for the user yet, it should walk them through doing it themselves.

Step 8 of 10

How I defined quality

I set concrete output criteria for the first release: no emojis, and tables rendered as tables. I also pushed for a short list of recommendations, three at a time with more on request, so users weren't overwhelmed.

One of the eval tools we used recorded the sentiment of customer conversations. It flagged when customers said things like "that's not it," "that's wrong," or "no, you're wrong," or typed in all caps. Those flags pointed us to the frustrated conversations, so we could read them, see why customers were frustrated, and find the pain points we needed to solve.

Honestly, the harder part of evals was pulling all those findings together and deciding what to fix first. We had to do that across teams, with PMs, engineers, and leadership, and weigh it against where everyone's priorities were.

Step 9 of 10

Outcome since GA

Customers were satisfied at GA, a clear step up from Marketing Agent, with formatting and polish work still ahead. My lead metric was adoption among customers at GA, paired with repeat use, because everyone had free credits for the first month and a raw count alone can mislead.

Both were positive and significant. The numbers aren't public, but I'm happy to walk through them in conversation.

Step 10 of 10

What I'd do differently

I'd bring people in earlier. We didn't realize how many teams would contribute to Composer once every team became its own agent, and figuring that out all at once was hard. I wish I had gathered designers, engineers, and PMs before it became a thing, to say: here's what's coming, your team will probably be affected, and I'd love to brainstorm what this could be with you. That way they'd have had input early, as a team, instead of a PM thinking of an idea later and then wanting to add it to Composer.
