Marketing Agent

As the main designer working with engineering, I helped take Klaviyo's Marketing Agent, an AI agent that generates campaigns, forms, and flows for marketers, from prototype to its K:BOS 2025 launch, and designed the jumpstart onboarding that got new customers up and running fast.

Role
Main designer, working with engineering
Team
Product designers, engineering
Timeline
May 2025 to launch at K:BOS 2025

Context

I was the main designer working with engineering on Marketing Agent. A team of designers helped visualize the product and create its happy path; I focused on the systems level.

That meant thinking through how new customers would get involved with Marketing Agent right from Klaviyo sign-up, what would happen when it didn't work, and what would happen when a user wanted to edit something. I worked through those use cases with engineering to get them shipped.

I started on Marketing Agent in May 2025, and it launched at K:BOS, Klaviyo's 2025 conference in Boston.

Launch scope

At launch, Marketing Agent focused on content generation rather than data. It generated campaigns, forms, and flows for customers, and there were two ways in.

Existing customers saw recommendations in line, in the table on the campaigns, forms, or flows page, saying "we built this for you." A second entry point sat in the top nav header. Customers sometimes discovered it, but the in-line recommendations got far more adoption.

New customers got a jumpstart path, built to get them up and running on Klaviyo as quickly as possible. They signed up from the login page on Klaviyo's website and went through a specialized onboarding that captured their brand colors, logo, and brand voice, which we used to curate generated campaigns, forms, and flows for them. This path worked really well: it didn't need much data about the customer, and the content was ready to go.

Problem and stakes

Marketers were spending hours building content, and they often didn't have ideas for what to send next. So we focused on helping brands come up with new campaign ideas and get them out to their customers quickly.

Customer voice

I talked to customers almost every other day, testing different prototypes and designs, and used self-service recruiting to get real users in front of them.

Showing where the content came from

Existing customers really wanted to know where Klaviyo was pulling information from to create their content. So we added a link in Marketing Agent that showed the source: the brand and colors area of the product.

Customers always wanted control

In my experience, about 95% of the time a customer wanted to edit whatever we created for them. They almost never just clicked send. Customers always wanted control, and that's a learning I took with me into Composer.

Placement

Marketing Agent had two entry points for existing customers: the top nav header, and recommendations in line, inside the tables where customers already work. We didn't set out to test them against each other, but usage told the story: in-line recommendations got far more adoption than the header. The exact numbers aren't public, but the difference was significant. That's why Composer appears where the work happens.

Constraints

Three constraints shaped the design most.

A fixed launch date

We wanted to launch at K:BOS and keep up with the AI race.

Content quality

We kept hearing that the quality of the generated content wasn't matching what customers wanted. In the UX, we pointed customers to the source, for example asking them to update their brand and colors, because that's where we pulled from. We also advocated for tracking the changes users made when editing, though that wasn't implemented.

Editing

Customers always wanted to edit, and to be taken straight to their Klaviyo editor, but the backend architecture couldn't support that. So we sent users to an intermediate screen showing what we'd generated for them, where they could send it right away or click Edit to open the editor.

Decisions

Decision 1: the agent proposes, the customer decides

The agent never acted on its own. It proposed content, and nothing was created in the customer's account until they chose what to do with it: clicking Edit created a draft, and clicking Send created a full campaign. Since customers wanted control almost every time, the customer always made the call.

Decision 2: an intermediate screen instead of the editor

Customers wanted to go straight into their Klaviyo editor, but the backend architecture couldn't open it directly, and we needed to ship for K:BOS. So instead of a full preview with options to revise or roll back, we built an intermediate screen showing what the agent had generated, with two choices: send it right away, or click Edit to open it in the editor. What we gave up was a seamless edit.

Decision 3: pull what we can, ask only when we must

A lot of designers swarmed on this project to help create its vision. I was the one who sat down with engineering, and with leadership. Leadership wanted a quick start for new customers, so I took on a whole new end-to-end flow for getting started with Klaviyo fast.

I worked through it with one engineer, just the two of us, going back and forth on what we could assume or pull automatically and what we needed the customer to confirm before we got started. For example, if a customer entered their website and we could tell it ran on Shopify, we skipped the onboarding step that asks which service hosts their site. We only asked when we couldn't tell. The onboarding was full of decisions like that, each one aimed at automating as much as we could for the customer.

When the AI was wrong

AI-generated content should be able to learn from a customer's past campaigns and see what they're actually sending. Instead, Marketing Agent pulled from Klaviyo's brand settings, and that's where it went wrong.

The two-year-old logo

Customers would get a generated campaign with their logo from two years ago, which was stressful to see. They had updated their logo on their campaign and email templates, but never in Klaviyo's brand settings, because whenever they created a campaign they cloned an existing one instead of visiting the brand section. Brand settings were exactly where we were pulling from. That's why the interface pointed customers back to the source, so they could update it.

The bar for AI-generated content isn't "good." It's faster than doing it myself, and that means working from what the customer is actually sending today.

How I defined quality

Our bar for good was simple: match the customer's brand. Customers liked the ideas Marketing Agent came up with, but when the content didn't match their brand colors, logo, or brand voice, for example slang or emojis they would never use, it simply wasn't good. That was the bar we used to craft the generations.

How we checked it

We added a simple thumbs up and thumbs down on generations, and tracked how many people clicked Send on the output versus going to edit. What we couldn't track was whether people who edited ended up sending the campaign, which made it really hard to keep reliable numbers.

Composer, which came next, was designed to give customers more control over what was generated, and over editing in general.

Outcome

Marketing Agent launched at K:BOS 2025. The jumpstart path got new customers up and running quickly, and in-line recommendations drew far more use than the header entry point, a positive and significant lift. The numbers aren't public, but I'm happy to walk through them in conversation.

Its biggest lessons, that customers always want control, that people engage where they already work, and that AI content has to match the brand, shaped how I approached Composer.

Lessons carried forward

  • Meet people in the work they're already doing. In-line beat the header, so Composer appears where the work happens.
  • Let the user lead. Marketing Agent decided what to make, while Composer starts with a prompt box where the user describes the campaign they want right now.
  • Quality comes before autonomy. If editing costs more time than creating, people will leave.

What I'd do differently

I'd push much earlier to learn from customers' past campaigns. At Klaviyo, we know customers clone their campaigns and templates when they send something out, so I'd build on those past templates. Starting from a customer's own template and focusing on generating the content and pulling in images would likely have cost Klaviyo less than generating everything from scratch. That's the biggest thing I'd do differently: lean on past campaigns over brand settings.
