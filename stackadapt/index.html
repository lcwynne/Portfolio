The vision and the restraint8 / 22

What I presented: a prompt box feature within the product, Klaviyo's version of a Claude-style assistant, anchored on the customer's data.

What I left out on purpose: my belief that people will always need some kind of interface (text counts, but many people look rather than read), and that the assistant should come to you instead of being a sidekick you go find.

Why I held it back: to avoid overpromising to the board. The sidekick was the fastest MVP, and getting the plumbing accurate mattered more than a new delivery model.

The board response: one member asked when it was coming, another pushed on transparency. I watched my product partner hold his tongue, and learned to underpromise, overdeliver, and keep room to learn.

The Klaviyo brain11 / 22

This was still a discussion about the right way to do it: how many agents, what each does, and how you know which does it best.

My anchor: one source of truth that fact-checks the others. Example: the campaign agent writes a subject line, and the segments agent checks it targets the audience that will move the numbers.

Left: several agents that each do one thing, with the Klaviyo brain fact-checking them. Right: one brain that somehow does all the work.

Product and others wanted one big Klaviyo agent. I agreed it was the destination, but argued the platform wasn't built for it: segments were built first, then campaigns, and they were never connected.

My call: fragmented mini agents with one brain on top as the bridge.

The long-term goal: fewer agents and one main agent, a chief of staff, carrying out small tasks.

Results16 / 22

Honest answer to "did I build everything I wanted to": not fully. Was it a decent MVP: yes.

We started with content generation because it was visual and the data was ready. Analyzing and connecting customer data wasn't, because the infrastructure didn't exist yet.

Content generation was used heavily. It also cost users credits, and cost Klaviyo credits. Users kept saying they wanted to see why something was generated before getting another pile of copy.

Cost changed the design: before credits mattered we pre-generated ideas and recommendations. As cost grew, I explored tiered, paid proactive recommendations and a mobile app for insights, like a paid chief of staff. Exploration only, not shipped.

Evals

Stop relying on thumbs up and thumbs down.

  • Read the sentiment in the chats themselves: what customers like and dislike, without asking them.
  • In the future: a continuous loop with data scientists, so that signal improves the model automatically, not through direct edits.
  • That loop is also how the chat gets personalized for each customer.
Results17 / 22

Thumbs up and thumbs down only tell you something when people bother to click them.

Instead: sentiment analysis on the chats themselves. A customer typing in all caps, "why did you generate this for me?", tells you more than a thumbs down ever will. Go through those conversations to see what customers like and dislike, based on how the chats are going, instead of relying on direct feedback.

In the future I want to work closer with data scientists, so that signal automatically improves the model, rather than us making direct edits. A continuous loop.

That loop is also how we end up personalizing the chat for each customer.

The message on screen is a recreated example, not a real customer.

What I'd do differently

  1. Get ahead of the teams building agents: bring them together early, and ideate with them.
  2. Start with the data analysis.
  3. Treat cost per generation as a design constraint from day one.
What I'd do differently18 / 22

I would try to get ahead of everyone else building these agents. I would bring teams together proactively and ask: what's your game plan here? If they don't have one, ideate with them on what this could be, and get them involved early.

A lot of teams were playing catch-up, and coming into the game late meant we had to backtrack on design decisions. They might not have all the answers, and that's okay.

In solving the problem, I would start with the data analysis.

And I would treat cost per generation as a design constraint from day one.

Then it is Claude Code or Figma. Claude Code comes first if I already have a prototype with ideas built into it that I can use, or if I am making a quick adjustment for a PR.

The recording is one of those PRs: users wanted to know what we were updating, so I pulled from an internal changelog and published it to Composer's public releases.

Figma comes when I need something pretty specific and I am looking at two different ideas.

Point: I choose the tool by the job, I do not use AI for everything.
